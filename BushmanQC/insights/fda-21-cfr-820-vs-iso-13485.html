FDA 21 CFR 820 vs ISO 13485: A Founder's Cheat Sheet

Two regulations, one job. Here's where the FDA's 21 CFR Part 820 and the international standard ISO 13485 overlap, where they diverge, and which one a medical device startup actually needs first.

By Nellie Bushman, Quality Expert & Founder, BushmanQC · Updated May 21, 2026

TL;DR

  • 21 CFR Part 820 is the FDA's quality system regulation for medical device manufacturers. ISO 13485:2016 is the international standard. They cover the same scope.
  • From February 2, 2026, the FDA's harmonized QMSR incorporates ISO 13485 by reference — so the two converge significantly.
  • If you sell in the US only and only ever will, 21 CFR 820 / QMSR is what you must satisfy. If you have any international ambition, build the QMS to ISO 13485 from day one — it covers both.
  • The two are not interchangeable in enforcement. The FDA inspects; notified bodies and registrars certify. The artifacts you produce are similar, but who shows up at your door is different.

What each one is

21 CFR Part 820 (FDA Quality System Regulation)

The US Food and Drug Administration's Quality System Regulation, codified in Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part 820. It is law in the United States. Medical device manufacturers selling in the US must comply, and the FDA enforces compliance through inspections. Findings are issued as Form 483 observations, escalated to Warning Letters, and ultimately to consent decrees or import alerts for serious failures.

21 CFR Part 820 was written in 1996 and has been in continuous use since. As of February 2026, it is replaced by the harmonized Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR) — which incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference and adds a thin layer of FDA-specific requirements. See our QMSR transition checklist for the migration plan.

ISO 13485:2016

An international standard from the International Organization for Standardization, last revised in 2016. It is not law anywhere — it is a voluntary consensus standard. But its certification is the practical entry ticket for medical device markets outside the US: the EU MDR/IVDR, UK MDR, Health Canada, Australian TGA, and most others rely on ISO 13485 as the QMS baseline.

Compliance with ISO 13485 is demonstrated by certification audits performed by accredited registrars (sometimes called certification bodies). Once certified, you must pass an annual surveillance audit and a full re-certification every three years.

Which one does my startup need?

Here is the decision tree we use with founders:

  1. US only, no international ambition. Implement to 21 CFR Part 820, then transition to QMSR by February 2026. This is the simplest path for a small Class I or Class II device intended for the US market only.
  2. US plus any other market. Implement to ISO 13485 from day one and operate the QMS in a way that also satisfies 21 CFR 820 / QMSR. Maintain one QMS, satisfy both inspection and certification.
  3. Non-US first. Implement to ISO 13485 from day one and pursue certification. You can layer FDA-specific requirements on later when you are ready to register for US distribution.

In practice, option 2 is what we recommend for most startups. ISO 13485 is broader than 21 CFR 820 in some places (supplier risk, training competence) and the FDA's QMSR explicitly incorporates ISO 13485 by reference from 2026. Building to ISO 13485 future-proofs your QMS in a way that building only to 21 CFR 820 does not.

Where the two genuinely differ

Enforcement model

The FDA inspects you. They show up, ask for your QMS records, talk to your people, observe operations, and document what they find. ISO 13485 is verified by a registrar's certification audit — usually scheduled, with a defined scope, against a documented audit plan.

The practical effect for a startup: an ISO 13485 audit is predictable and surveys breadth. An FDA inspection is less predictable, narrower in scope at any given moment, and far deeper on the subsystems chosen. CAPA is the most common deep-dive target.

Complaints and adverse events

21 CFR 820 requires complaint handling to feed into the FDA's Medical Device Reporting (MDR) system, with specific timelines and content for events that meet the reporting threshold. ISO 13485 requires complaint handling but does not prescribe the reporting pathway — that comes from local regulation (EU MDR, MHRA, etc.). A QMS built to satisfy both must have complaint records that cleanly link into multiple regulatory reporting pathways.

Records retention

21 CFR 820 sets minimum retention durations explicitly. ISO 13485 leaves it to the manufacturer to define based on the device's expected lifetime and applicable regulation. A dual-purpose QMS uses the longer of the two: lifetime of the device or 2 years post-distribution, whichever is longer.

Supplier risk

ISO 13485 has a more developed treatment of supplier risk in clause 7.4 — requirements scale with the criticality of what the supplier provides. 21 CFR 820 takes a flatter, more uniform approach to supplier controls. A dual-purpose supplier program follows the ISO 13485 risk-based model, which also satisfies FDA.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between 21 CFR 820 and ISO 13485?

21 CFR Part 820 is the United States FDA's Quality System Regulation for medical device manufacturers. ISO 13485 is an international standard for medical device quality management systems. They cover the same scope but with different structure, vocabulary, and enforcement model. After February 2026, the FDA's harmonized QMSR incorporates ISO 13485 by reference.

Do I need both 21 CFR 820 and ISO 13485?

If you sell medical devices in the United States, you must comply with 21 CFR Part 820 (becoming QMSR in February 2026). If you sell in the EU, UK, Canada, Australia, or most other regulated markets, ISO 13485 certification is the practical requirement. Most medical device companies with global ambitions implement a single QMS that satisfies both — far simpler than maintaining two.

Is ISO 13485 certification required by the FDA?

No. The FDA does not require ISO 13485 certification. The FDA inspects against 21 CFR Part 820 (or QMSR from February 2026). However, once QMSR is in effect, the FDA's regulation incorporates ISO 13485 by reference, so an ISO 13485-aligned QMS makes FDA compliance simpler.

Is the FDA Quality System Regulation the same as QSR?

Yes — "QSR" is shorthand for the FDA's Quality System Regulation, i.e. 21 CFR Part 820. From February 2026, the FDA replaces QSR terminology with QMSR (Quality Management System Regulation), the harmonized rule.

How long does it take to implement a QMS that satisfies both?

For a small startup medical device company with no existing QMS, the BushmanQC Virtual QMS implementation typically takes 8–16 weeks to a fully usable, audit-ready state. The dual-purpose nature of the implementation does not extend the timeline meaningfully versus a single-standard implementation.
