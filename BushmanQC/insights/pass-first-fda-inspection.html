How to Pass Your First FDA Inspection

The pre-inspection checklist, what FDA investigators actually look at on day one, and the top five Form 483 observations every first-time medical device inspectee should know how to avoid.

By Nellie Bushman, Quality Expert & Founder, BushmanQC · Updated May 21, 2026

TL;DR

  • A first inspection lasts 3 to 5 business days. The investigator opens with management responsibility and quickly moves into CAPA, complaints, and design controls.
  • The top 5 inspectional observations year after year are CAPA, complaint handling, design controls, document control, and purchasing controls. Build to defend these and you've defended ~80% of the surface.
  • The single most common reason a first inspection escalates is not the substance of a finding — it's how the company responds in the room. Train your front-of-house people to be helpful, honest, and bounded.
  • If you don't already have a written inspection-readiness procedure, you do not have an FDA-ready QMS. The procedure is itself an inspection record.

What happens during an FDA inspection

For a small medical device manufacturer, an FDA inspection is conducted by one or two investigators from the FDA's Office of Regulatory Affairs. The inspection follows a documented protocol — typically the Quality System Inspection Technique (QSIT) — that focuses on the most consequential subsystems: management responsibility, design controls, CAPA, and production/process controls. The investigator selects a sample of records under each, follows the threads, and documents what they find.

The inspection ends with a closing meeting. If the investigator has observations, they are presented on Form 483 — a numbered list of findings. You have 15 business days to respond in writing with a corrective action plan. A clean inspection ends without a 483 issued.

Day-by-day: what a 5-day inspection looks like

Day 1 — Opening, management responsibility

The investigator presents credentials and the FDA Form 482 (Notice of Inspection). In the opening meeting, they explain the scope, the protocol, and what records they'll need. They then ask for the management responsibility records: quality policy, quality objectives, management review minutes, organization chart, and the responsibility/authority matrix.

What they're testing: does this company have a real, lived management system, or is the QMS a binder on a shelf? If management review meetings happen monthly with substance, with action items tracked and closed, you've passed the first day.

Day 2 — CAPA

The investigator asks for the CAPA log, then samples 3 to 5 CAPAs and audits each end to end. For each one: was the issue defined clearly? Was the investigation timely and proportionate? Was the root cause identified, not just the symptom? Was a corrective action taken? Was its effectiveness verified? Did the verification actually happen, or is it a checkbox?

CAPA is the most consequential subsystem in any first inspection. A weak CAPA program is the single most common reason an inspection goes badly.

Day 3 — Design controls

The investigator picks one product and audits the design history file (DHF) end to end: design inputs, design outputs, design verification, design validation, design reviews, design transfer to production, and design changes. They are looking for traceability — every input traced to a verification, every change traced to a re-verification.

Day 4 — Complaints, MDRs, production controls

The investigator audits the complaint log against MDR records, looking for complaints that should have been reported as MDRs but weren't. They review production records, process validations, and (if relevant) sterilization or other special process records.

Day 5 — Closing

Follow-up on threads from earlier days. The investigator drafts the 483 (if any) and presents it in the closing meeting. You have the right to discuss observations before they're finalized — use that right.

The top 5 Form 483 observations to avoid

1. CAPA — inadequate procedure or ineffective program

The most cited observation, year after year. The common failure modes: CAPAs that don't have clear problem statements, root cause analyses that stop at the symptom, corrective actions that aren't tied to the root cause, and "effectiveness verifications" that are an email saying "looks good."

How to avoid it: write a CAPA procedure that requires (a) a measurable problem statement, (b) a documented root cause analysis using a defined method (5 whys, fishbone, fault tree), (c) a corrective action that addresses the root cause specifically, and (d) effectiveness verification with objective evidence at a stated future date. Train every CAPA owner on it.

2. Complaint handling — failure to investigate, failure to file MDRs

Complaints that come in and never close. Complaints that should have been reported as Medical Device Reports but weren't. Investigations that are one sentence: "no defect found."

How to avoid it: every complaint goes through a defined intake → triage → investigation → resolution flow. The triage step explicitly decides whether the complaint meets the MDR reporting threshold and, if it does, records that decision and the MDR submission. Aged complaints get escalated.

3. Design controls — incomplete or out-of-date DHF

Design changes that happened in engineering but never made it into the DHF. Verification protocols that don't trace back to the design inputs they're supposed to verify. Design transfer records that exist but skip steps.

How to avoid it: a design-change procedure that requires every change — including changes that occur after design transfer — to be evaluated for impact, re-verified or re-validated where required, and documented in the DHF. A traceability matrix from inputs to verifications, maintained as a living document.

4. Document control — uncontrolled documents in use

A work instruction taped to a wall in the production area that's an older version than the one in the QMS. A drawing in someone's local folder that's been edited but never re-issued. Forms filled out on revisions that have since been superseded.

How to avoid it: a document-control procedure with a controlled distribution model (digital is easier than paper), explicit removal of obsolete documents from points of use, and a periodic audit of the production area for uncontrolled documents.

5. Purchasing controls — supplier qualification gaps

Critical components purchased from suppliers that were never formally qualified. Supplier audits that were planned but never executed. Receiving inspection records that don't tie back to the purchase specification.

How to avoid it: a supplier-evaluation procedure that risk-classifies suppliers, defines qualification requirements per class, and triggers re-evaluation at defined intervals or events. Use the ISO 13485 risk-based model — it satisfies QMSR as well.

The pre-inspection checklist

Run this checklist 60 days before any planned inspection — and proactively if you've never been inspected, because the FDA can show up unannounced.

Documents

  • Quality Manual current and approved.
  • Quality Policy and quality objectives posted/communicated.
  • Organization chart with named individuals current.
  • Management Representative formally appointed in writing.
  • Master document list current; no superseded documents at points of use.

Records

  • Management review minutes for the last 12 months, with action items closed.
  • Internal audit schedule covering every subsystem in the last 12 months, with findings and CAPAs.
  • CAPA log: every open CAPA has a current owner and target date; no CAPAs aged beyond their target without justification.
  • Complaint log: every complaint resolved or with a documented reason it isn't.
  • Training records: every employee trained on the procedures relevant to their role, training documented and effective.

Inspection logistics

  • Inspection-readiness procedure written and approved.
  • Front-of-house staff (receptionist, office manager) briefed on what to do when FDA arrives.
  • "War room" identified — a private space the investigator can work in.
  • Document retrieval workflow tested: from any record cited in a 21 CFR 820 subpart, can your team retrieve the underlying record within 5 minutes?
  • Designated scribe: a named person whose only job during inspection is to take detailed notes.

The five rules of behavior during inspection

The substance of your QMS matters. So does how your people behave in the room. Here are the rules we train every BushmanQC client on:

  1. Be honest. The investigator will catch a lie. Lies escalate inspections faster than any substantive finding.
  2. Be helpful, not voluntary. Answer questions accurately. Do not volunteer information beyond what was asked. "I don't know — let me find out" is a complete answer.
  3. One voice. One designated person — typically the management representative or quality lead — speaks to the investigator. Other staff are present only when asked.
  4. Slow down. Take time to find the right record. The investigator would rather wait 10 minutes for an accurate document than receive the wrong one in 2 minutes.
  5. Document everything. The scribe captures every question asked, every record requested, every observation made aloud. This becomes the basis for your post-inspection response.

After the inspection

If you receive a Form 483, you have 15 business days to respond in writing. A strong response:

  • Acknowledges each observation specifically.
  • Describes the root cause your investigation identified — not the symptom.
  • States the corrective action and the date by which it will be complete.
  • Commits to verifying effectiveness, with a verification date.
  • Provides supporting evidence (revised procedures, training records, etc.) where already available.

A 483 that gets a clear, dated, evidence-backed response within 15 business days is the most common path to no escalation. A late, vague, or defensive response is the most common path to a Warning Letter.

Frequently asked questions

How long does an FDA inspection take?

A first-time inspection of a small medical device manufacturer typically lasts 3 to 5 business days. For-cause inspections triggered by a complaint or adverse event can be longer. Pre-approval inspections for a PMA can take a week or more.

What is a Form 483?

FDA Form 483 is the formal list of inspectional observations issued at the close of an inspection. Each observation describes a condition the investigator believes is a violation of 21 CFR Part 820 (or QMSR after February 2026). Receiving a 483 is not a finding of guilt; how you respond determines whether it escalates to a Warning Letter or beyond.

What are the most common FDA 483 observations?

Year after year, the top observations cluster around CAPA (inadequate procedures, untimely investigations, ineffective verification of corrections), complaint handling, design controls, document control, and purchasing controls. CAPA-related observations alone account for roughly a quarter of all 483s issued to medical device manufacturers.

Does the FDA give advance notice of an inspection?

For routine inspections, the FDA typically gives 5 business days' notice to a US manufacturer. For-cause inspections — those triggered by a complaint, adverse event, or other regulatory concern — can be unannounced. Pre-approval inspections for PMAs are coordinated with the submission timeline.

Can I refuse to let an FDA investigator in?

Practically, no. Refusing access invites a far more serious escalation, including possible court orders. The right approach is to welcome the investigator and operate your inspection-readiness procedure.
