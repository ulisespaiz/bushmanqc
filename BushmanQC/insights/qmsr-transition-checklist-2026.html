QMSR Transition Checklist for 2026

The FDA's Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR) replaces 21 CFR Part 820 on February 2, 2026. Here's a month-by-month plan to be ready — what changes, what carries over, and the order a small medical device company should tackle it in.

By Nellie Bushman, Quality Expert & Founder, BushmanQC · Updated May 21, 2026

TL;DR

  • QMSR (21 CFR 820, as amended) goes into effect February 2, 2026 and incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference, plus a small set of FDA-specific additions.
  • If you already operate to ISO 13485, ~80% of your QMS is QMSR-ready. The remaining 20% is mostly relabeling, a few definitions, and how complaints, records, and management responsibility tie back to FDA submissions.
  • If you operate to 21 CFR 820 only, expect a 4–6 month transition for a small startup — most of which is documentation and training, not new processes.
  • Do not wait for your next FDA inspection to discover gaps. Build a written transition plan, close gaps in phases, and document the bridge.

What QMSR actually changes

The biggest single change is that QMSR incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference. The FDA is no longer maintaining a parallel, US-specific quality regulation — it has chosen to align with the international standard and add a thin layer of FDA-specific requirements on top.

For a medical device company already certified to ISO 13485, the work is mostly cosmetic: relabel your QMS scope, confirm your complaint-handling and records procedures meet the FDA-specific additions, and make sure your management-responsibility documentation reflects the regulation as it now reads. For a US-only company that has only ever operated to 21 CFR Part 820, more work is needed — but the new requirements are not radically different, just structured differently.

Areas that are essentially unchanged

  • Design controls (Subpart C of 820 → Clause 7.3 of ISO 13485, near-identical scope).
  • Document control and records retention.
  • CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action) — still the most-inspected subsystem.
  • Production and process controls, including process validation.
  • Supplier evaluation and purchasing controls.

Areas that change in form, not substance

  • Management responsibility. The wording aligns with ISO 13485's clause 5; the substantive expectations are similar but the documentation must explicitly reference QMSR.
  • Risk management. QMSR makes ISO 14971 a more explicit reference point. If you weren't already managing risk to 14971, you should be by February 2026.
  • Training and competence. ISO 13485 language replaces 820's language; the practical effect on a startup is small.

Areas with genuine new requirements

  • Complaint handling and records of investigations. FDA-specific requirements layer on top of ISO 13485's clause 8.2.2 — every complaint that is also a reportable event must be documented as such, with the link to MDR records preserved.
  • Records availability for inspection. The regulation explicitly addresses how QMS records are made available during an FDA inspection — there is no equivalent ISO 13485 clause.
  • Labeling and unique device identification (UDI). QMSR preserves the US-specific UDI requirements that are not in ISO 13485.

The month-by-month transition plan

This is the plan we recommend for a small medical device company with an existing 21 CFR 820 QMS, starting six months before the QMSR effective date. Larger companies follow the same shape but at a faster cadence with more parallel workstreams.

Month 1 — Gap assessment

  • Compare your existing QMS procedures, line by line, against ISO 13485:2016 and the QMSR-specific additions.
  • Catalogue every procedure, work instruction, form, and record. For each, mark: no change, language change only, or substantive change.
  • Identify the owners for each substantive change.
  • Write the transition plan as a controlled document in your QMS — this is itself an FDA-inspectable record.

Month 2 — Procedure updates

  • Update procedures in dependency order: top-level Quality Manual first, then management responsibility, then the eight ISO 13485 macro-clauses (resource management, product realization, measurement-analysis-improvement, etc.).
  • Cross-reference every internal procedure to the new clause numbers.
  • Process all updates through your document-control process — do not bypass change control just because the change is "regulatory."

Month 3 — Complaints, records, and MDR linkage

  • Update your complaint-handling procedure to the QMSR-specific wording. Confirm the linkage between complaint records, investigation records, and Medical Device Reports (MDRs) is documented end to end.
  • Refresh your records-availability procedure to address the QMSR-specific inspection-readiness expectations.
  • Verify ISO 14971 risk-management linkage from design through post-market — many companies have a gap here because risk records were not updated when product changed.

Month 4 — Training

  • Train every employee whose role touches a QMS procedure. Document the training delivery, the materials used, and the assessment of effectiveness.
  • Update your training-needs analysis to reflect the new procedure set. New hires must be trained to QMSR from their first day on.

Month 5 — Internal audit

  • Conduct a full internal audit against the updated QMS. The audit plan should cover every clause of ISO 13485 plus the QMSR additions.
  • Issue findings, raise CAPAs where required, close them.
  • Review at management review meeting.

Month 6 — Final readiness

  • Mock FDA inspection. Have someone outside your quality function — ideally an external consultant — run a one-day mock inspection against the new regulation.
  • Close any remaining gaps. Update your transition plan to "complete" with evidence.
  • You are ready for QMSR.

The cheapest mistake to avoid

Treating the QMSR transition as a one-time documentation update. The FDA's enforcement model rewards evidence of an effective system, not the existence of paperwork. If your training records show every employee was "trained" on the new procedures in a one-hour all-hands, your first inspection under QMSR will go badly.

The companies that pass inspections cleanly are the ones whose training records, internal audits, management reviews, and CAPAs all show the new regulation being lived day to day. The plan above is structured to build that evidence as a byproduct of the transition, not as a separate exercise.

Frequently asked questions

When does QMSR take effect?

The FDA's Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR) is effective February 2, 2026. From that date, 21 CFR Part 820 is replaced with the harmonized regulation that incorporates ISO 13485 by reference.

Is QMSR the same as ISO 13485?

QMSR incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference and adds a small set of FDA-specific requirements — notably around complaint handling, records of investigations, and the relationship between management responsibility and US regulatory submissions. It is not identical to ISO 13485, but a company already certified to ISO 13485 has done the bulk of the work.

Do I need to recertify under QMSR?

There is no FDA certification under QMSR — the FDA inspects against the regulation. What you need is documented evidence that your QMS satisfies the new requirements before your next FDA inspection after February 2026.

What if my product is in design when QMSR takes effect?

Your design controls must satisfy the regulation as it stands at the time of each design activity. In practice this means design records started before February 2026 against 21 CFR 820 are fine; design activities continuing into 2026 should be performed under the new procedures and cross-referenced.

Does QMSR affect my 510(k) or PMA submissions?

QMSR governs the manufacturer's quality system, not the device's regulatory pathway. Your 510(k) or PMA process is unchanged — but the QMS records you cite in those submissions must reflect QMSR-compliant procedures by February 2026.
