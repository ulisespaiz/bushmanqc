How to Choose a Medical Device QMS Consultant (2026 Buyer's Guide)

What a QMS consultant does, when a startup needs one, how the work is priced, and the questions that separate an expert from a template reseller.

By Nellie Bushman, Quality Expert & Founder Published October 8, 2026 Last reviewed October 8, 2026 ~12 min read

Short answer: Choose a QMS consultant who has built medical device quality systems under the QMSR and ISO 13485 for companies of your size and device class, who names the person doing the work, and who leaves your team owning and able to run the system. For most startups the right shape is a fixed-scope project to stand up the QMS, then a small fractional retainer to keep it current. Bring them in before design controls start, not the month before a submission.

Most founders hire their first quality consultant the way they hire their first lawyer: under deadline pressure, off a single referral, without a clear picture of what good looks like. This guide is the checklist we'd want every founder to have before that first call. It covers what a QMS consultant actually does, when a startup needs one, how the work is priced, the questions that separate an expert from a template reseller, and where a Virtual QMS fits.

What does a medical device QMS consultant actually do?

A medical device QMS consultant designs, builds, fixes, or helps run the quality management system FDA requires under 21 CFR Part 820. Since February 2, 2026, that regulation has been the Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR)1, and it requires every manufacturer in scope to document a quality management system that complies with ISO 13485 plus FDA's US-specific requirements, under 21 CFR 820.10(a)2. In practice, consulting work falls into four buckets:

  • Build. Write the Quality Manual, procedures, forms and logs; set up document control, training records, CAPA, supplier controls and design controls; configure the tools that hold it all.
  • Assess. Run a gap assessment against the QMSR and ISO 13485, or a mock inspection before an FDA investigator or a registrar arrives.
  • Fix. Remediate findings from an audit, a Form FDA 483, or a system that was built for a much bigger company.
  • Sustain. Operate parts of the system part-time: review document changes, keep training current, move CAPAs forward, prepare management review.

What a consultant cannot do is own your QMS. The regulation holds the manufacturer responsible, and § 820.10(e) makes any person responsible for a failure to comply subject to regulatory action2. ISO 13485, which the QMSR incorporates by reference, requires top management to appoint a member of management as management representative in clause 5.5.2 (you can read the standard free in the ANSI incorporation-by-reference reading room3). A good consultant will push you to name an internal owner on day one instead of quietly filling that seat themselves.

When does a startup need a QMS consultant?

Not the day you incorporate, and not the week before a submission. Four trigger points cover most startups, and each one calls for a different kind of help.

1. Before design controls start

This is the highest-leverage moment to bring someone in. Design and development requirements (ISO 13485 clause 7.3) apply to every class II and class III device, plus class I devices automated with computer software and five listed class I device types, under § 820.10(c)2. The records you create while designing become your design and development file (DDF), formerly the Design History File (DHF), and they can't be honestly recreated later. What you need here is a lightweight system: document control, training, design controls and risk management, and not much else. Our guide to design controls for founders covers what those records are.

2. Before a 510(k) or De Novo submission

Now you need someone who can confirm that design verification, validation, risk management and design review records exist and trace to each other, and who can get the rest of the QMS (CAPA, complaint handling, supplier controls, production controls) operating rather than merely written before you ship finished devices. Clearance is not a quality system audit, but once you are manufacturing for sale, the full QMSR applies and you can be inspected. Our QMSR checklist is a good self-test before you hire.

3. Before an ISO 13485 certification audit

If Canada, the EU or other markets are on your roadmap, you'll need a certificate from an accredited registrar. FDA has said it will not require or issue ISO 13485 certificates, and that a certificate does not exempt a manufacturer from FDA inspection4, so this is a separate business decision. Look for a consultant who has prepared companies for registrar audits specifically, not just FDA inspections. The differences are covered in 21 CFR 820 vs ISO 13485.

4. After a Form 483 or a failed audit

A Form FDA 483 lists an investigator's observations. FDA says it is not a final Agency determination, and it encourages companies to respond in writing with a corrective action plan5. Observations that aren't fixed can escalate to a Warning Letter6, which FDA publishes. At this stage you want a remediation specialist who does real root cause analysis and systemic correction, not someone who rewrites the cited procedure and moves on. See Quality System Remediation and our first FDA inspection walkthrough.

Eight criteria for choosing a QMS consultant

1. Medical device experience at your class and pathway

Quality experience from pharma, aerospace or general ISO 9001 work transfers only partly. Ask for medical device systems they've built for companies of your size, device class and regulatory pathway.

2. Current on the QMSR, not the old QSR

FDA retired its QSIT inspection approach on February 2, 2026 and now inspects under Compliance Program 7382.8504. A consultant still writing procedures around old section numbers such as § 820.30 is building to a regulation that no longer exists. Ask them to explain § 820.10, § 820.35 and § 820.45 in plain English.

3. Right-sized for your team

Every procedure is a promise an investigator can check. A five-person company running fifty procedures will break them. The right consultant asks who will operate the system before they write anything.

4. A real plan for Part 11 and software validation

If your records are electronic, 21 CFR Part 119 applies, and the software that holds your QMS needs validation proportionate to its risk. FDA's final Computer Software Assurance guidance10 (February 2026) describes a risk-based way to do that. Your consultant should be able to tell you what the validation record will look like. Our Part 11 guide lists the controls.

5. A named person doing the work

Get the name of the person who will write your procedures and sit in your audits, and make it part of the contract.

6. You own the deliverables, in portable formats

Procedures, forms and records should be yours outright and exportable. If the system only works inside a consultant's proprietary platform, you're renting your QMS.

7. Knowledge transfer

The engagement should end with your team trained to run document control, CAPA and training records without calling the consultant for every change.

8. Independence, or honest disclosure

If a consultant earns referral fees from a software vendor, you should know before they recommend that vendor.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • Which medical device QMS implementations have you done for companies of our size and device class, and can we speak with one of them?
  • What changes did you make to your procedures for the QMSR, and when?
  • Who exactly will do the work, and how many hours a week can they give us?
  • What is the complete list of deliverables, and what does "done" mean for each one?
  • How will our electronic records and signatures meet Part 11, and who writes the validation record?
  • How many procedures do you expect we'll need at our stage, and why that number?
  • What happens after go-live: training, maintenance, audit support, and at what rate?
  • Do we own everything you produce, and can it move to another tool if we change platforms?
  • Do you have any financial relationship with the software you're recommending?
  • How will you get our team to run this without you?

Red flags

  • A guarantee of FDA clearance or a clean inspection. No one can promise either.
  • Old QSR language in a 2026 proposal, or no mention of the QMSR at all.
  • A template set delivered with your logo swapped in, and no discovery of how your company actually works.
  • "Part 11 certified" software offered as the whole Part 11 answer. Part 11 places its controls on the people and companies that use a system, not just the product.
  • An enterprise-sized system for a pre-revenue team, with more procedures than people to follow them.
  • No written scope, or no named practitioner.
  • An offer to be your management representative instead of helping you appoint one.

Rule of thumb: The best consultant engagement ends with a system your own team can run on an ordinary Tuesday. If you'd be lost without the consultant six months after go-live, the system was built for them, not for you.

Where a Virtual QMS fits

A Virtual QMS is one specific answer to the "build" question: a complete quality management system kept in compliant cloud storage, approved with Part 11 electronic signatures, and organized so a small, distributed team can operate it without paper or an office. It suits startups that need a real, inspectable QMS before they need, or can justify, enterprise eQMS software or a full-time quality department.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need eQMS software before we hire a consultant?

No. Decide who will build and run the system first, then choose the tool. Buying software before you've designed your processes usually means configuring it twice. Our decision guide covers when software makes sense.

Can a consultant be our management representative?

ISO 13485 clause 5.5.2 calls for a member of management to fill the role3. In a startup that is usually a founder or an early operations leader, supported by the consultant.

Is a template kit enough?

Only if someone on your team already knows how to tailor it. Templates move the hard part, fitting the system to how you actually design and build, onto you.

How long does it take to build a startup QMS?

It depends on scope and how much documentation already exists. BushmanQC's Virtual QMS implementations typically take 8 to 16 weeks; an FDA-ready system for a later-stage company with production and suppliers takes longer than a design-stage starter system.
